01 · Problem Discovery

Range Anxiety is a Data Problem,
Not a Battery Problem

When I joined JLR's connected services team in 2022, the company had just launched its first fully electric platform. The hardware was genuinely competitive: 300-plus miles of real-world range, fast-charge capability, and excellent thermal management. Yet post-purchase survey data showed that range anxiety remained the single most cited barrier to customer satisfaction, rated 4.1 out of 5 in severity by I-PACE and early Defender PHEV owners.

The problem was not the battery. It was the information system around the battery. Existing in-car navigation treated battery state as a static variable: it showed remaining range as a circle on a map and offered a single "nearest charger" recommendation. It did not account for elevation change, traffic-induced energy drain, charger occupancy, dynamic electricity pricing, or the driver's personal comfort threshold for arriving with a low state of charge. It treated a fundamentally dynamic, multi-variable logistics problem as a simple distance calculation.

I do not want to know that I have 180 miles of range. I want to know if I can make it to my in-laws in Sheffield, accounting for the M1 at 70 mph, the hills, and the fact that I want to arrive with at least 20% charge so I do not spend the whole drive panicking. That is not what the current system tells me.
Composite user quote drawn from 12 discovery interviews

The insight that reframed the entire product brief: range anxiety is a decision support failure, not a hardware failure. Users already had enough range for most of their actual journeys. What they lacked was a planning interface that helped them trust that range confidently. This positioned the problem as a data and UX problem, directly analogous to how a travel booking platform like Skyscanner helps a traveller trust a layover connection time, or how a logistics platform gives a driver confidence in a delivery window. The PM skills required were not automotive-specific. They were universal.

02 · User Research & HCI

Research Methodology

I designed and led a three-phase research programme. Phase one was 12 moderated discovery interviews conducted via video and two in-home sessions with I-PACE owners, using a semi-structured discussion guide. Phase two was a contextual inquiry study: four participants drove a defined 140-mile route while I observed and noted every moment they checked the battery display or expressed uncertainty. Phase three was Figma prototype usability testing with 18 participants using think-aloud protocol and System Usability Scale scoring.

Think-Aloud Protocol: Key Findings

During think-aloud sessions with the existing navigation prototype, participants verbalised the following behaviours that we captured as design requirements:

  • 01
    The "Good Enough" Override Problem: 11 of 18 participants dismissed the system-recommended charger and manually searched for alternatives because they did not trust the system's recommendation. The system showed the nearest charger; participants wanted the best charger for their specific situation. Trust was absent because the recommendation had no explanation.
  • 02
    The Arrival Anxiety Spike: All 4 contextual inquiry participants showed measurably increased check frequency on the battery display in the final 40 miles of a route when estimated range margin fell below 30 miles. This corroborated survey data showing anxiety peaks in the final third of a journey, not at departure. The insight: the planning interface must give users a continuous confidence signal throughout the journey, not just a pre-trip recommendation.
  • 03
    The Cost Blindspot: Zero out of 12 discovery interview participants could accurately estimate what their last charging session cost. Users were making stop decisions with no pricing information. This represented a direct product gap: integrating real-time tariff data into the route planner was both technically feasible and clearly valued by the Efficiency Maximiser segment.

I ran two rounds of moderated usability testing using Figma prototypes: Round 1 tested the existing navigation interface (baseline), and Round 2 tested the Smart Trip Planner prototype with 18 participants each. Usability Research Finding

The largest single SUS improvement between Round 1 and Round 2 came from adding a plain-language explanation beneath the recommended charger stop: "Recommended because it is on your route, has 3 of 4 bays available, and saves you 18 minutes vs. the nearest alternative." Users who saw this explanation accepted the recommendation at a 79% rate, versus 34% for the unexplained recommendation. This became Acceptance Criteria 3 in the core PRD and was shipped in Sprint 2.

Vehicle Telemetry Analysis: Finding Range Anxiety Hotspots

The connected vehicle platform emitted anonymised, aggregated telemetry events per trip. I worked with the data engineering team to model a schema representing trip segments, and wrote a series of SQL queries to identify which route corridors and trip profiles were most associated with unplanned charging stops, which we defined as a stop not within 1.2 miles of a pre-route charger.

Query 1 · Unplanned Charging Stops by Route Corridor
WITH trip_segments AS (
  SELECT
    trip_id,
    vehicle_id,
    segment_id,
    start_soc_pct,
    end_soc_pct,
    distance_km,
    avg_speed_kmh,
    elevation_gain_m,
    ambient_temp_c,
    hvac_draw_kw,
    grid_ref_start,           -- H3 hexagon index for spatial grouping
    planned_stop_flag
  FROM telemetry.trip_segments
  WHERE trip_date BETWEEN '2022-06-01' AND '2022-12-31'
    AND distance_km > 80          -- Only long-haul trips where range matters
),
unplanned_stops AS (
  SELECT
    cs.trip_id,
    cs.charger_lat,
    cs.charger_lon,
    cs.stop_timestamp,
    ts.grid_ref_start,
    ts.end_soc_pct            AS soc_at_stop
  FROM telemetry.charging_stops cs
  JOIN trip_segments ts ON cs.trip_id = ts.trip_id
  WHERE cs.planned_stop_flag = 0
    AND cs.soc_at_stop_pct < 18   -- Stop with critically low charge (anxiety event)
)
SELECT
  grid_ref_start                        AS route_hex,
  COUNT(*)                              AS unplanned_stop_count,
  AVG(soc_at_stop)                       AS avg_soc_at_unplanned_stop,
  COUNT(*) * 1.0 / COUNT(DISTINCT trip_id) AS stops_per_trip_ratio
FROM unplanned_stops
GROUP BY grid_ref_start
HAVING COUNT(*) >= 10
ORDER BY unplanned_stop_count DESC
LIMIT 25
-- Maps to Tableau hex choropleth: anxiety hotspot corridors by H3 cell
Query 2 · Range Prediction Accuracy by Segment Conditions
SELECT
  CASE
    WHEN avg_speed_kmh < 80  THEN 'Urban / Slow'
    WHEN avg_speed_kmh < 110 THEN 'Motorway Cruise'
    ELSE                          'High-Speed (>110)'
  END                                     AS speed_band,
  ROUND(AVG(ambient_temp_c))              AS avg_temp_c,
  AVG(predicted_range_km)                 AS predicted_range_km,
  AVG(actual_range_km)                    AS actual_range_km,
  AVG(actual_range_km - predicted_range_km)AS prediction_error_km,
  STDDEV(actual_range_km - predicted_range_km) AS error_stddev,
  COUNT(*)                                AS segment_count
FROM telemetry.trip_segments
WHERE predicted_range_km IS NOT NULL
GROUP BY 1
ORDER BY ABS(AVG(actual_range_km - predicted_range_km)) DESC
-- Reveals that high-speed motorway driving under-predicted actual consumption by avg 31 km
Telemetry Insight That Reframed Engineering Scope

The range prediction accuracy query revealed that at motorway speeds above 110 km/h, the existing EPA-based range model over-predicted range by an average of 31 km. This single finding was the data justification for building a real-time, physics-based range model that ingested live speed, temperature, HVAC draw, and elevation data. It also explained why anxious users on the M1 were chronically panicking: the system was telling them they had more range than they actually did.

A/B Test Statistical Summary

Variant B produced a +35.8% relative improvement in trip success rate (67% to 91%) and a +92.7% relative improvement in charger recommendation acceptance (41% to 79%). Both results were statistically significant at 99.1% confidence(approx.), calculated using a chi-squared test on binary outcome data. The test ran for 8 weeks to control for seasonal confounds and weekday versus weekend trip pattern differences. We shipped Variant B globally as the default experience in the next software-over-the-air update.

04 · Product Execution

PRDs, Sprint Planning, and the
Prioritisation Decisions That Shaped the Build

PRD User Stories: Core Feature Set

I wrote five user stories for the MVP feature set. Below is the most consequential one, which directly addressed the primary finding from user research: the need for a driver-controlled arrival safety margin.

User Story · PRD Excerpt · Smart EV Trip PlannerEVTP-US-04 · Sprint 2

Feature: Minimum Arrival State-of-Charge Preference

As a Range Anxious driver planning a 180-mile trip to an unfamiliar destination,

I want to set a minimum state of charge I am comfortable arriving with (e.g., 20%), so that the system plans my charging stops conservatively and I do not spend the final hour of the drive panicking,

So that I can drive with confidence and focus on the road rather than the battery display.

  • AC1 A slider control in Trip Settings allows the driver to set minimum arrival SoC between 5% and 40% in 5% increments. Default value is 15% for new users, adjustable in profile settings.
  • AC2 The route planning algorithm treats the minimum arrival SoC as a hard constraint: no route is surfaced that results in predicted arrival SoC below this threshold under worst-case conditions (10th percentile energy consumption for that route profile).
  • AC3 If the driver's preferred arrival SoC requires an additional charging stop that adds more than 25 minutes to journey time, the system surfaces a comparison: "Conservative plan (25 min longer) vs. Standard plan (arrive with 12%)." Driver selects preferred option.
  • AC4 During navigation, the in-trip confidence bar displays current projected arrival SoC in real time. If projected SoC drops below the driver's threshold due to changed conditions (higher speed, headwind), the system proactively suggests rerouting to an earlier charger.
  • AC5 The system learns from driver behaviour: if the driver consistently overrides the conservative setting and arrives with higher SoC than required, it surfaces a suggestion to lower the threshold by 5% after three consecutive trips. Requires driver confirmation before adjusting.
  • AC6 All SoC projections display the underlying assumption (e.g., "Based on 65 mph average, 12C ambient, HVAC on"). Driver can expand to see sensitivity: how SoC changes at 75 mph vs. 65 mph.

Hardest Prioritisation Decision: What to Build First

The engineering team estimated the full feature set at 34 sprint points across three capabilities: (A) battery preconditioning on route (automatically warming the battery for rapid charging 20 minutes before arrival at a fast charger), (B) dynamic rerouting for pricing (suggesting a cheaper charger if a significantly lower-cost option opens up en route), and (C) integrated in-app payment (paying for charging directly from the JLR app without a separate network app). All three were valued by users. I had engineering capacity for one in Sprint 1.

Post-launch, the product team at JLR integrated Smart Trip Planner engagement as a leading indicator in the quarterly connected services NPS review. Owners who used the planner on more than 60% of their long-distance trips (defined as over 80 miles) showed a Net Promoter Score of 72(approx.), versus 41 for non-users of the feature. The feature also reduced inbound calls to the owner helpline about "range issues" by 38%(approx.) in the six months post-launch, representing a meaningful reduction in support cost.

The dynamic pricing rerouting feature was adopted by 58% of active users within 90 days of launch(approx.), particularly within the Efficiency Maximiser segment. Average charging cost per mile dropped by approximately 12%(approx.) for users who accepted rerouting suggestions, creating a measurable, personal financial benefit that directly served the value proposition: this is not just less anxious driving, it is materially cheaper driving.

Transferable Skills: Why This Is Not Just an Automotive Case Study

Every skill demonstrated in this case study maps cleanly to adjacent domains. The range polygon is a real-time confidence interval rendered spatially, which is the same engineering primitive as a delivery ETA window on a logistics platform or a flight delay probability model on a travel app. The charger recommendation engine is a multi-criteria ranking problem with user preference constraints, identical to hotel or flight sorting on a booking platform. The battery preconditioning feature is a pre-emptive resource allocation problem, directly analogous to pre-loading data for an IoT device before a connectivity window, or pre-heating a building zone before occupancy in an energy management system.

Domain-Agnostic PM Skill Summary

The human-centred design methods (think-aloud protocol, SUS scoring, journey mapping) transfer verbatim to any consumer product domain. The telemetry SQL patterns apply directly to any IoT or connected device platform. The RICE-driven prioritisation methodology is domain-neutral. The A/B test design is identical in structure to any digital product experiment. And the core insight, that a user's problem is not a hardware constraint but a decision support failure, is the kind of reframing that defines strong product thinking in any sector.

What I Would Do Differently

  • 01
    Instrument the anxiety signal directly, not as a proxy: I used unplanned charging stops as a proxy for range anxiety events. In hindsight, I would have instrumented the in-car display check frequency (how often the driver tapped the battery icon) as a direct behavioural signal. This data existed in the telemetry stream but was not modelled. A higher check frequency is a real-time anxiety signal and would have made the cohort analysis richer.
  • 02
    Ship the companion app feature parity earlier: The home planning feature on mobile lagged the in-car experience by one full release cycle (approximately 10 weeks). During that gap, users who planned on mobile and then entered the car found the plan was not synced, which generated disproportionate support contacts. A feature flag approach to ship mobile planning in a limited beta would have prevented this.
  • 03
    Involve energy grid data partners earlier in PRD review: The Octopus Energy API integration revealed a data latency issue (tariff updates with a 4-hour lag) only during Sprint 3 technical design review. Had I included the partner integration lead in the Sprint 1 PRD review, we would have designed the pricing display with an explicit "as of" timestamp from the start, avoiding a mid-sprint scope change to the HMI design.
